Benchmarks

Everything on this page is reproducible from the repository, and all full-suite results are our own runs, not upstream's published tables. Every shootout cell was re-measured at v0.4.0 with five replicates per hash; the digest is unchanged since v0.4.0, so the numbers describe the current release (v0.4.5).

Hosts
Apple M1 P-core, ~3.2 GHz, bare metal, Apple clang -O3 -mcpu=native. Fanless machine, so its figures are medians over replicates.

EPYC 7B13 (Zen 3), EPYC 9655 (Zen 5) shared KVM guests with no frequency control; absolute rates not comparable, archived as indicative. Their within-host ratios reproduce the conclusions below: the 9655 gives the same 1.99× bulk lead over ChibiHash v2 as bare-metal Zen 5, and the Zen 3 host, with no AVX-512, collapses it to 1.05×.

Caveats that matter before reading the table

  • * The AVX-512 condition. hayahash's 31.2 B/cy needs the compiler to auto-vectorize the bulk loop, which requires AVX-512DQ for its packed 64-bit multiply. A separate five-replicate sweep on the same host gives 31.1 B/cy under GCC, 31.1 under Clang, and 17.6 under GCC without AVX-512DQ — behind rapidhash's 28.6. An EPYC 9655 reproduces the same 1.8× gap under GCC 13 and Clang 18; an EPYC 7B13 with no AVX-512 at all cannot reach the vectorized shape however it is tuned. The source stays portable scalar C either way — nothing is hand-vectorized — but a machine without AVX-512 gets the lower rate. Small-key figures are unaffected.
  • The small-key column is dependent latency, not throughput. SMHasher3 feeds each hash's output into the next key, so hashes cannot overlap. It is not comparable to the independent-hash figures in the ChibiHash tables below.
  • Calibration correction. SMHasher3 measures call overhead once per process and subtracts it from all 31 lengths; that calibration varied by ~2 cycles between runs, which is 10–40% on Zen 5. After correcting it, the five Zen 5 replicates agree to within 0.35 cycles. The archived records in paper/ report both raw and corrected figures.
  • gxhash is Zen 5 only. Its SMHasher3 port takes the hardware path solely under x86 AES; an ARM number would describe a software-AES fallback rather than gxhash.

What the table says, by instruction class

  • Wide multiply: rapidhash v3 passes and stays ahead of hayahash on small keys everywhere, and on both axes on the M1. It remains the honest recommendation when a native 128-bit multiply result is available — exactly the instruction hayahash's portability rules exclude. Its Zen 5 bulk lead did not survive hayahash's vectorizable bulk loop: 28.6 B/cy against 31.2, and only where that loop vectorizes.
  • SIMD or hardware AES: where the hardware provides them, these win bulk decisively. XXH3-64 reaches 48.9 B/cy on Zen 5 and 12.6 on the M1; gxhash reaches 64.7 on Zen 5. Both fail the suite, but they are not beaten on speed.
  • Portable 64-bit scalar: the only other hash here that meets hayahash's constraints and passes is ChibiHash v2. hayahash leads it in bulk on both hosts, 1.61× on the M1 and 1.99× on Zen 5. Small keys split: hayahash takes 11% less time on the M1 but 30% more on Zen 5, where ChibiHash v2's simpler short path wins the 8–15-byte band by about 5.3 cycles. mx3.v3, the remaining ordinary-multiply candidate, fails 26 tests.

Taken together: within the portable scalar class, hayahash is the fastest hash we found in sustained bulk that passes the complete SMHasher3 suite, on both hosts. On Zen 5 it also beats every wide-multiply hash measured here in bulk — conditional on the bulk loop vectorizing; without AVX-512DQ it falls to 17.6 B/cy, behind rapidhash. And the claim is deliberately scoped, not a universal record: two bare-metal hosts, bounded by the roughly 250 hashes SMHasher3 tracks.

Head-to-head: ChibiHash

ChibiHash sets out to do a similar thing — portable, ordinary multiply — which makes it the most useful baseline. hayahash is its own design, not a fork; where the header borrows a specific trick, it says so. v1's 8/16-byte latency wins come from special-cased paths that are also part of why it fails SMHasher3. Among the two functions that pass, hayahash is fastest at every size here. The 32–128-byte rows reflect a deliberate dispatch choice on clang targets: give up 5–9% at fixed sizes to run 2–10% faster on mixed-size workloads, which single-size tables cannot show. The optimization log documents that trade.

On this host hayahash now leads both ChibiHash versions at every measured size. The 320–512-byte band, which v2 led before the fifth optimization pass, flipped to a 27–47% hayahash lead, and sustained bulk roughly doubled once GCC learned to auto-vectorize the bulk loop for AVX-512. Dispatch shapes are tuned per architecture and compiler; every shape produces identical digests.

Reproduce it

# ChibiHash head-to-head (references vendored in tests/)
make -C tests run-bench

# SMHasher3, pinned to an exact upstream commit
make -C tests/smhasher3 run

# Baseline-wasm32 shootout and wasm-vs-native bit-exactness
make -C tests/wasm run-kat run-bench

smhasher3.md covers running the suite, covering all dispatch shapes, and re-deriving verification values. paper/ holds the archived raw runs behind every number on this page.
